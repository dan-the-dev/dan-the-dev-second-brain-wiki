## XP Techniques for Collective Ownership

# Summary

### Where does typical organizational problems comes from?
- Developers are typically the person that knows how the organization is going more than everyone else, but they are never listened to.
- A lot of today’s organizational processes that does not work well, and the high control existing in the companies, comes from the lack of technical practices that XP identified more than 20 years ago
- Example: Disfunctional PO, bad sprint planning, non predictability of a team, management control, gantt - are all control system that are there not because people is evil, and not because managers don’t study the right things, but because the lack of technical practices creates a code that push in that direction
- Problems: bad architecture, bad code design, low collective ownership, non socio-collaborative approach to coding - and all of these stuff generates control
- Current processes are not important, because those are the consequences of current technical practices - processes will improve naturally when technical practices will be better and the code and architecture will improve
- Typical symptom: you add people but speed is the same

### How to improve things and tell people to improve
- Go working with the software development team
- A technique to make problems emerge in front of people:
    - work 2/4 weeks in pair/mob with the team
    - design the current architecture
    - show the architecture to the CEO and ask to explain with names given by tech team: all he can’t explain typically is something that shouldn’t be there
- Better to talk about inconsistency than technical debt: tech debt is seen as an accusation - we want instead to achieve consistency between architecture, company organization and company business - when those 3 things are inconsistent, organization slow down and cannot scale - and problems start
- Changing business is a 1-hour decision, changing organization maybe a month, changing architecture takes months/years - and architecture always wins over organization
- So better to start by changing the architecture so that the other 2 will follow easily

### Where to start from and how to ensure that things will improve
- Start from XP practices, first of all Pair/Mob Programming
    - With pair/mob, over time, you naturally build a collective ownership
    - We lose bottlenecks because we don’t depend on a single person - and this already seems like we are now faster
- Test automation
    - only way to have a easy to change product
    - only way to keep the cost of change under control
- If the team is big, we start talking about architecture - the idea takes from a paper of [Microsoft about Microsoft Vista](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/ownership.pdf), where it’s highlighted that collective ownership is far more impactful than writing bad code
- With commits, you can identify the point of architecture where there are more people committing - those are the areas with higher risk of tech debt, because the more people is touching a codebase, the more faster tech debt will grow
- Then you can introduce cross-functional teams

### Shared codebases: how to go out of the problem
- Split codebases by products
- If you really need to share a codebase between different teams, you need to have very strict rules on it

### XP for processes
- Flexible iteration are useful so that the team can think about it and adapt it

### How to grow an organization with XP
- First of all, is it really needed to grow for the company?
- Typically, an XP team stay small and is effective in the same way as a bigger team not doing scrum
- Are we sure that companies grow for a reason or they just grow accidentally? For example, we think that just adding people makes us faster, but instead it slow us down - while if we improve our technical practices we actually go faster with the same people
- The target is to de-scale the organization, not to scale it → if you can do something with less people, it is better

### Introducing agility works best from the top or from the bottom?
- They are similar, what we can tell for sure is that the worst situation is when it comes from the middle (middle managers) because you have to push on both directions
- When it comes from the bottom, it’s ideal because you can work on technical practices and then start delivery continuously to proof that control is useless
- Even if it comes from the bottom, anyway, we have to be careful to not just force a new process - you need to change technical practices before to enable a new way of work before

### Samples of failures
- If the company is not ready to transparency (agility requires full transparency, example is business not trusting estimates) or the company has only external suppliers for software - in such contexts agility becomes almost impossibile
- Also, if everything is external, it is a proof that the company doesn’t value software enough
- Another example from his experience: a company where the top level wanted agility, but the team at the bottom didn’t accept the changes required to enable it

### New landscape of management
- We are living the first examples of new young management that wanted agility when they were devs and now they want it from their team / or young management from young startup that are agile/lean from the start
- In this case, it was the team that wasn’t interested in changing their technical practices to improve architecture and software

### XP mentality
- XP don’t want you to be obsessed with the perfect situation, you have to accept the ideal idea of working with XP practices but also be aware that it might happens that sometimes is not possible - and be fine with it
