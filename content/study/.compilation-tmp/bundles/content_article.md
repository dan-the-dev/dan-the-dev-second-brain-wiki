## Introduction to Excellent Engineering Teams
- The author of the document "okay" vs excellent engineering teams discusses the differences between average and excellent engineering teams, based on their 15+ years of experience in tech and 7 years of managing engineering teams.
- According to the authors of the book Peopleware, an excellent team, also referred to as a 'jelled' team, is characterized by a sense of joint ownership of the product, low turnover, a sense of eliteness, and obvious enjoyment in their work, with team members feeling like they are part of something unique.
- The author disagrees with the idea that teams cannot be made to 'jell', and instead believes that engineering managers can build excellent teams by implementing a few small habits, such as fixing the root cause of problems rather than just patching them, and giving engineers ownership of specific areas or 'kingdoms'.

## Characteristics of Excellent Teams vs. 'Okay' Teams
- The author notes that 'okay' teams tend to patch problems rather than fixing the root cause, which can lead to a significant amount of time being wasted on repeated fixes, and that excellent teams, on the other hand, know when to fix the root cause and prioritize these fixes based on their potential return on investment.
- In excellent teams, engineers are given ownership of specific areas, such as applications or microservices, and are responsible for making decisions and prioritizing work, rather than just executing tasks assigned to them by the engineering manager.
- The author emphasizes that building an excellent team is not about working twice as hard or having only '10x engineers', but rather about developing a few key habits and giving engineers the autonomy to own and make decisions about their work.

## Ownership and Autonomy in Excellent Teams
- The author also mentions the importance of killing tiny annoyances, such as alerts or flaky tests, which can have a high return on investment and free up time for more important work, and notes that excellent teams have a debate and make concrete decisions, rather than just following inertia.
- The key to excellent engineering teams is to give engineers the authority to make real decisions, not just responsibility, as this leads to a sense of ownership and connection to their work, which is essential for their growth and challenge.
- Excellent teams prioritize unblocking other teams first, putting their needs before their own 90% of the time, which helps build a reputation as a team that always helps others and creates a more joyful experience for their engineers.

## Collaboration and Decision-Making in Excellent Teams
- Unlike okay teams that simply execute the roadmap, excellent teams shape it by partnering with product managers to decide what gets built, talking to customers, and understanding the business, which allows them to take ownership and push back on features that don't make sense.
- Excellent teams are willing to kill the plan if necessary, and before starting work, they ask critical questions such as how to measure success, define criteria for continuation, and what would make them decide not to continue with a feature.
- Excellent teams treat deployment as the halfway point, not the finish line, and they ask questions such as whether users are actually using the feature, if it's moving the desired metric, and if there are any friction points that need to be addressed.

## Technical Practices and Prioritization in Excellent Teams
- Excellent teams treat tech debt as product work, giving technical initiatives a clearly defined business value, and prioritizing them alongside other product initiatives, rather than treating them as a separate backlog or a 20% tax.
- The engineer who owns a project makes decisions, monitors its health, works closely with the product manager, and is the go-to person for other teams, which is a key characteristic of excellent teams, and this approach helps prevent engineers from feeling unchallenged and unhappy, which is a top reason why they quit.

## Role of Engineering and Product Managers
- The engineering manager (EM) needs to effectively communicate the business impact of technical work to the team, providing explanations that go beyond just stating the need for a task, such as refactoring a monolith, and instead explaining how it will benefit the team, for example, by enabling them to ship features three times faster without being blocked by other teams.
- A strong partnership between the engineering manager (EM) and the product manager (PM) is crucial for building an excellent engineering team, and this partnership is a key factor in achieving the team's goals and objectives.

# Strong domain knowledge, understanding requirements, good relationship with stakeholders
It takes time to learng in-depth knowledge of any business. 80/90% of a topic can be learned and lived in a couple years, bue a real Subject Matter Expert have seen exceptions and edge cases, understanding implication of certain requirements and their impact.

Leading a team typically means managing poor performers, star performers, assigning increment and cutting bonuses. It also means managing expectations, negotiating budgets and timelines, explanation of lapses, fighting for your team during moderation.

> *When a user says she wants “A”, the lead would know that it is actually “A, B, and C” and that in order to achieve that, “D, E, F, G, and H” will need to be done.*


# Understanding your team’s strengths and weaknesses
Being aware of the team’s strength and weaknesses makes you able to understand if a project can be done by the team itself, which risks are on the table, give more accurate estimates and understand if some up-skilling budget or resources are needed. This will allow you to avoid to over-promise and under-deliver.

An advice: focus on strengths, not weaknesses; each person is unique and have strengths and weaknesses, it’s ok, and strengths are it’s natural inclination so you should help him exploit it and grow it.


# Understanding that it is more productive to up-skill your team than to expand it
***Quality over quantity.***

Let’s explain this in numbers: imagine you have 1000 man/days of work to be done and your team is made of 5 people. You cannot reasonably finish within the timeline.

It’s way more preferable to improve team capabilities in order to be able to do the same amount of work in 900 man/days from the same people.

Adding new people typically slow down the team in the short period and create a “storming” phase where the team must re-organize itself.


# Deciding on technology to use for the project
The world is full of technology. As a tech lead, you should understand as many of them, and use the one that best suit for the job in the long-term.

The language must be able to support business expansion, business changes, integrate with other technology used.


# Establishing standards and framework
Standard way of working makes it easier for everyone to understand and explain how things work in the code. Is make easier for a dev to take on the in-progress work of another.

Establishing a standard typically includes industry standards and best practices, keeping high quality in code and implementations.


# Mentoring and guiding teams
Growing your team to be more capable and be a high performer is your job. Making sure that they have time for self-development is also one of the important roles played by the leader.


# For a new project, be very involved and hands-on
At least at the start of a project, it is important that the technical lead ensures that the technical architecture and design are properly planned and implemented.


# Make firm decisions
Decisions have to be made via a lot of thinking through a lot of things: business requirements, expected user behaviour, business processes, scenario permutations and its frequency of occurrences, market practices and standards, cost-benefit analyses of requirements, risk of building (or not building) certain features within timeline, technical dependencies, business needs and prioritization (and potential changes), and finally, quality and capacity of your team.

Once decided, stand by it unless it was later discovered that there are fundamental mistakes in the assumptions, then change as fast and possible.


# Take ownership, fending the team, give credits
> *The first mistake is always that of the leader’s.*

As a technical lead, know that if there are technical issues in the project, you are the first one accountable.

What is important to note here is that even small bugs can be traced to wrong decisions at the root and they are usually that of the leader. It is then your job to recognize this and fend the team against the negative outburst from stakeholders if major issues happened within the project.

And lastly, give proper credits to the team. Be sincere and specific when doing so. The work is not only done by you.


# Respect Others
A narcissistic boss is one of the most damaging personalities that a company can have in the organization. Yes, they may charm people with their charisma and technical know-how, but they damage a team’s productivity and team’s contributions usually far outweighs that of any individual.


# Un-Bonus: Technical Expertise
Truth be told, technical expertise isn’t really a real requirement for a technical leader role. Not in a big organization anyway. While it is important for a technical lead to understand technology, best practices, etc. as I’ve stated above, it is actually not critical for a lead to be a God-like developer. This is a point that many developers failed to realize. In fact, tech leads can be technically poorer in coding than people in the team, their job is to make sure that people who are better than them can do their work without unnecessary distractions (e.g. handling troublesome clients, dealing with management presentation, etc.). And if someone in the team is not performing, find out why and take responsibility for bettering that person.

**Remember this: a smart leader hires people who are better than them.**

# Che cos'è una feature factory?

## Summary

The concept of a "feature factory" refers to a company where product development is treated like an assembly line of features, with managers deciding on features, designers embellishing them, developers building them, and then launching and piling them on top of each other without focusing on whether they are helping customers.

*(sintesi auto-estratta dal contenuto catturato — da rifinire)*

## Key Insights

Detailed summary

- The concept of a "feature factory" refers to a company where product development is treated like an assembly line of features, with managers deciding on features, designers embellishing them, developers building them, and then launching and piling them on top of each other without focusing on whether they are helping customers.
- A feature factory is characterized by a lack of attention to metrics, such as whether the latest feature launched is being used by anyone, and what numbers the company wants to improve with the new functionality.
- Some key indicators that a company is a feature factory include: no attention to metrics, no refinement of features after launch, no redesign of inconvenient features, features are never eliminated, work is done in separate phases, features are promised to reach commercial agreements, and no failures are anticipated.
- In a feature factory, the focus is on creating new and shiny features without considering their purpose or impact on customers, and the company often works in isolation, with different teams passing the ball to each other without collaborating.
- The opposite of a feature factory is a team that is outcome-oriented, has autonomy, and works together towards creating an impact on customers, with the creation of new features being just one of the means to achieve this goal.
- [[john-cutler|John Cutler]], who coined the term "feature factory", highlights the importance of considering the impact of features on customers and the need for teams to work together, taking into account metrics and customer needs.
- The author, Pietro Campagnano, a strategic consultant, notes that he has seen companies benefit from refining and redesigning features, and that eliminating features that are not working can also be beneficial, and he encourages readers to check out John Cutler's original article for more insights.

## Introduction to Technical Debt Concept

- The term "technical debt" is a metaphor that was first used by [[ward-cunningham|Ward Cunningham]], who described it as the result of writing code without a proper understanding of the underlying objects, leading to a disagreement between the business needs and the way the software is written.
- According to Cunningham, technical debt is not primarily caused by poor code quality, but rather by the disagreement between the business needs and the software design, which can arise from two main reasons: Wrong Design, where the solution was flawed from the beginning, and Rapid Evolution, where the solution was correct but became obsolete due to rapid changes in the landscape.

## Misconceptions About Avoiding Technical Debt
- The idea that technical debt can be avoided by writing clean code from the start is not entirely accurate, as even with the best intentions and practices, technical debt can still accumulate, and it is essential to understand the underlying causes of technical debt to effectively address it.
- The management and development teams can use the concept of technical debt as a lever to negotiate priorities, deadlines, and resources, but it is crucial to have a clear understanding of what technical debt entails and how it arises, rather than just using it as a buzzword.

## Root Causes of Technical Debt
- To reduce technical debt, it is essential to identify the root causes, such as Wrong Design or Rapid Evolution, and address them accordingly, which may involve re-evaluating the solution and making necessary changes to align it with the current business needs and landscape.
- The technical debt is often caused by a lack of understanding between the business requirements and the way the software is written, which can occur even when the development team has sufficient skills, but rather due to a misunderstanding of what needs to be done.

## Design Phase Strategies
- To address this issue, one approach is to invest more time in the design phase, which involves a more in-depth analysis by the developers and more discussions with stakeholders to understand both the current and future evolution of the project, in order to find the right balance or "sweet spot" between design effort and technical debt.
- However, spending too much time on the design phase can weaken the results over time, and in cases where the business environment is changing rapidly, a more agile approach may be necessary, which involves a cycle of rush, learn, and refactor, where the focus is on writing code quickly, learning as much as possible about the business reality, and then refactoring the code to consolidate and improve it.

## Agile Approach to Managing Technical Debt
- This approach accepts the creation of technical debt, but with the intention of paying it back in the short term, and it involves writing code that is clean and easy to refactor, learning and analyzing what works and what needs to be changed, and dedicating regular time to refactoring and improving the code.

## Rush, Learn, and Refactor Cycle
- The rush, learn, and refactor cycle can be summarized in three key elements: writing code that is clean and easy to refactor, learning and analyzing what works and what needs to be changed, and dedicating regular time to refactoring and improving the code, which can help to reduce technical debt and make the development process more sustainable.

- Ultimately, the focus should be on achieving the desired outcome, and humans should be involved in the loop to ensure that the software meets the required external quality standards, including functional, non-functional, and operational quality, rather than just leaving it to agents.

## Challenges of Agent-Driven Development
- The concept of "humans in the loop" in software engineering refers to the involvement of human developers in the lowest levels of the development process, where they can understand and fix broken code quickly, but this approach can become a bottleneck as agents can generate code faster than humans can manually inspect it.
- To improve the efficiency of the development process, it is suggested to adopt a "shift left" thinking approach, where agents are given the ability to gauge the quality of the code they produce themselves, rather than relying on humans to check it for them, and humans provide guidance on what they are looking for and how to achieve it.
- The collection of specifications, quality checks, and workflow guidance that control different levels of loops inside the development process is referred to as the agent's harness, and the practice of building and maintaining these harnesses is called Harness Engineering, which is how humans work "on the loop" rather than "in the loop".

## The 'On the Loop' Approach and Harness Engineering
- The "on the loop" approach involves humans defining the development process and the agent running it, and when the agent produces unsatisfactory results, humans change the harness to improve the outcomes, rather than fixing the artefact directly, allowing for continuous improvement of the development process.
- The next level of development is the "agentic flywheel", where humans direct agents to manage and improve the harness, by providing them with the necessary information to evaluate the performance of the loop, and feeding them richer signals, such as tests, evaluations, and operational data, to broaden the scope and depth of what the agents can analyze.

## The Agentic Flywheel and Continuous Improvement

# The Developer Productivity Trap

## Summary

The software industry has been attempting to measure developer productivity for decades, but every attempt to reduce it to a number has resulted in perverse incentives, gaming, and surrogation, where people focus on the metric itself rather than what it is supposed to represent.

*(sintesi auto-estratta dal contenuto catturato — da rifinire)*

## Key Insights

Detailed summary


## The Failure of Traditional Productivity Metrics
- The software industry has been attempting to measure developer productivity for decades, but every attempt to reduce it to a number has resulted in perverse incentives, gaming, and surrogation, where people focus on the metric itself rather than what it is supposed to represent.
- Various metrics, such as lines of code, story points, and velocity charts, have been used to measure productivity, but they have all been misapplied and have failed to accurately capture the complex and creative nature of software development.
- The use of lines of code as a metric is particularly problematic, as it can encourage developers to write sloppy and bloated code, and it does not account for the value created by the code, as illustrated by Bill Atkinson's experience with rewriting QuickDraw's region calculation engine for the Apple Lisa.
- Story points and velocity were originally designed as planning tools, but they have been misused as productivity metrics, leading to teams inflating their estimates and focusing on effort rather than value, which can result in broken trust, frustration, and culture damage.

## The Role of AI in Amplifying Productivity Measurement Issues
- The introduction of AI coding assistants has amplified the existing problems with productivity metrics, and research suggests that AI-assisted development is not a solution to the productivity trap, but rather a amplification of existing good and bad practices.
- The pursuit of a single metric to capture developer productivity is fundamentally flawed, and a better approach would be to ask a different question, one that focuses on the value created by developers rather than trying to measure their productivity through a single number.
- Experts such as Bill Gates and [[martin-fowler-software-engineer|Martin Fowler]] have spoken out against the use of lines of code as a metric, with Gates comparing it to measuring an airplane's progress by its weight, and Fowler noting that well-designed code is shorter because it eliminates duplication.

## Theoretical Frameworks: McNamara Fallacy, Goodhart's Law, and Surrogation
- The use of metrics such as story points and velocity has been criticized for focusing on effort rather than value, and for leading to predictable problems such as teams inflating their estimates and focusing on the wrong goals.
- The article concludes that the solution to the developer productivity trap is not to find a better metric, but to ask a better question, one that focuses on the value created by developers and the outcomes they achieve, rather than trying to measure their productivity through a single number.
- Cal Newport's research on "deep work" highlights the importance of sustained focus periods of 90 or more minutes for complex problem-solving, and measuring hours worked is not an effective way to evaluate contribution, as presence is a poor proxy for it.
- The McNamara Fallacy, named after U.S. Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara, describes a four-step process where easily measurable metrics are prioritized, and non-measurable aspects are disregarded, leading to a distorted view of progress, as seen in the Vietnam War where enemy body counts were used as the primary measure of success.
- [[goodhart-s-law|Goodhart's Law]] states that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure, and this is evident in software engineering where developers and teams manipulate metrics, such as deployment frequency and resolution rates, to meet targets, ultimately degrading the system.
- Surrogation is a concept that describes the nonconscious process by which people start treating a metric as the goal, rather than a proxy for the goal, due to attribute substitution, where the brain swaps the abstract goal with the concrete metric, and this can occur even without tying compensation to the metric.
- Research by Black, Meservy, Tayler, and Williams has shown that simply tracking and reporting a metric can trigger surrogation, and Harris and Tayler have identified three conditions that create surrogation risk, which are often met in software engineering, including abstract strategic objectives, concrete and visible metrics, and people accepting the measure as representing the goal.
- The combination of the McNamara Fallacy, Goodhart's Law, and surrogation explains why any single metric used for evaluation will eventually be gamed, surrogated, or both, and this is a predictable consequence of how human minds interact with numbers, rather than a flaw in specific metrics.

## DORA Metrics and Their Limitations
- Despite the challenges, the industry has produced better thinking about developer productivity, but it is often applied in the same old ways, highlighting the need for a new approach to evaluation and measurement in software engineering.
- The DORA metrics, introduced by Dr. [[nicole-forsgren|Nicole Forsgren]], Jez Humble, and Gene Kim in 2018, measure the outcomes of software development, including Deployment Frequency, Lead Time for Changes, Mean Time to Recovery, and Change Failure Rate, but are not intended for team-by-team comparison.
- Despite this warning, leadership teams often use DORA metrics to benchmark teams, which can be misleading as these metrics do not capture important aspects such as developer satisfaction, cognitive load, code quality, technical debt, or business value.
- The 2025 DORA Report acknowledged the limitations of the original metrics by introducing seven team archetypes, which reflect a more nuanced understanding of performance, and the SPACE framework, developed by Nicole Forsgren and colleagues, addresses the multi-dimensional nature of productivity across five dimensions.

## Criticism of the McKinsey Framework and Its Consequences
- Dr. Nicole Forsgren warned against misusing productivity metrics, such as focusing solely on developer activity, which can lead to burnout and decreased happiness, and instead emphasized the importance of examining a constellation of metrics to understand developer productivity.
- A recent strand of thinking focuses on the developer experience, highlighting the importance of feedback loops, cognitive load, and flow state in shaping developer productivity, and argues that improving these conditions is crucial for achieving better outcomes.
- The McKinsey report "Yes, you can measure software developer productivity" was widely criticized by the engineering community, including [[kent-beck|Kent Beck]] and Dave Farley, who argued that the framework was flawed and would lead to negative consequences, such as surveillance and decreased safety for developers.
- The critics argued that the McKinsey framework only measures effort or output, not outcomes and impact, and that introducing such a framework would be wrong-headed and likely to backfire, highlighting the need for a more nuanced and evidence-based approach to measuring developer productivity.

## The Manufacturing Metaphor and Knowledge Work Complexity
- The implementation of a framework to measure developer productivity can ultimately do more harm than good to organizations and their engineering culture, as it can lead to surrogation, where the metric becomes the target, and people manipulate it to achieve better scores, rather than focusing on actual developer satisfaction and productivity.
- The example of Facebook's developer satisfaction surveys illustrates this issue, where the introduction of an overall score led to managers pressuring engineers to achieve better scores, resulting in worse organizational outcomes despite improved metrics, as the metric became a target and was gamed by the employees.
- The tension between management consulting's desire for simple numbers to enable comparison and control, and the complex, collaborative, and creative nature of software engineering, is a major challenge in measuring developer productivity, as most measurement frameworks were designed for manufacturing, not knowledge work.
- According to [[peter-drucker|Peter Drucker]], knowledge work, such as software development, is fundamentally different from manufacturing, as it requires workers to control the task, own the means of production, and focus on effectiveness, making it difficult to measure productivity using traditional metrics.
- The manufacturing metaphor fails for software development because output is not standardized, efficiency is not the primary constraint, quality is not independently measurable, and the work itself is creative, requiring problem-solving, idea generation, and anticipatory imagination.

## Output vs. Outcome in Developer Productivity
- [[martin-fowler-software-engineer|Martin Fowler]] emphasizes the importance of distinguishing between output, which is what is produced, and outcome, which is the change that the output creates, and argues that the goal should be to minimize output and maximize outcome and impact, rather than focusing solely on output metrics.
- Fowler also notes that the best solution is often the smallest change, the feature not built, or the code deleted, which can be invisible or penalized under output-based metrics, and that organizations prefer the comfort of output metrics because they are immediate and controllable, even if they measure the wrong thing.
- [[charity-majors|Charity Majors]], CTO of Honeycomb, highlights that some of the most impactful engineering work can be invisible on individual metrics, and that the work that keeps a team effective, such as pairing, mobbing, and mentoring, can be difficult to measure using traditional output metrics, as it may show up as reduced personal output or "two people doing one person's job".
- The concept of measuring developer productivity through metrics is flawed, as it fails to account for essential tasks such as architectural decisions, cross-team coordination, and technical debt reduction that do not produce immediate visible results, but are crucial for the team's success, as noted by Majors and Dave Farley.

## The Dangers of Measuring Individual Developer Productivity
- Measuring individual developer productivity can have harmful effects, including distorting behavior, creating misaligned incentives, rendering collaborative work invisible, and damaging team culture, leading to unnecessary anxiety, overworking, and developer burnout.
- The introduction of artificial intelligence (AI) in software development has added complexity to the measurement of productivity, with studies on AI-assisted coding productivity yielding contradictory results, making it difficult to rely on traditional metrics, and leaders should treat these numbers with skepticism, as warned by experts.

## AI's Impact on Productivity Metrics and Surrogation Risks
- The use of AI in coding can lead to a perception gap, where developers believe AI is helping them more than it actually is, and this can create an illusion of effectiveness, making it challenging for leaders to make informed decisions about AI investment.
- The integration of AI in software development can exacerbate existing problems with metrics, such as gaming, perverse incentives, and surrogation, as AI can generate large amounts of code, create numerous pull requests, and ship constantly, without considering the actual quality or impact of the code, leading to codebase bloat, technical debt, and decreased product quality.
- Experts, including the CEO of Cursor, a company that sells an AI coding tool, are urging caution and warning about the potential risks of relying too heavily on AI-generated code, highlighting the need for careful review and consideration of the long-term impact of AI on software development.
- The adoption of AI tools in software development can lead to a phenomenon known as surrogation, where metrics such as "AI adoption rate" or "percentage of code generated by AI" become the primary goal, rather than the actual goal of improving engineering outcomes, as stated in the context of [[goodhart-s-law|Goodhart's Law]] and the 2025 DORA Report, which notes that "AI doesn't fix a team; it amplifies what's already there."

## Shifting Focus to Conditions Enabling Productivity
- AI is not a productivity solution, but rather a productivity amplifier that can exacerbate existing problems if applied to bad metrics, and therefore, leaders should focus on measuring the conditions that enable production, such as feedback loops, cognitive load, and flow state, as these factors are crucial in determining developer effectiveness.
- The most effective approach to improving developer productivity is to stop measuring what developers produce and start measuring the conditions that enable production, including factors such as how quickly developers get feedback, how much mental effort the development environment demands, and how often developers can achieve sustained, uninterrupted focus, as recommended by experts like [[kent-beck|Kent Beck]], who suggests focusing on "producing at least one customer-facing thing per team, per week."
- Research by Gergely Orosz and Abi Noda has found that leading teams use a mix of organization-specific qualitative and quantitative metrics, rather than adopting a framework off the shelf, and Google's approach to developer productivity measurement has shown that qualitative metrics, such as flow state, codebase navigability, and technical debt perception, are essential in capturing what automated systems cannot, and can only be measured by asking people.

## Qualitative Metrics and Feedback Loops
- Practical recommendations from the research include starting with qualitative baselines to identify opportunities, then deploying targeted quantitative metrics for deeper analysis, as this approach allows organizations to build their own understanding of what "good" looks like in their specific context, and to make data-driven decisions that align with their goals and values.
- The key to improving developer productivity is to segment by team and persona, rather than aggregating company-wide, and to prioritize free-text comments that can identify gaps and suggest improvements that structured questions may miss.
- It is essential to use transactional surveys at workflow touchpoints to gather granular and timely feedback, and to act on what is learned, as ignoring feedback can quickly kill a survey program, a concept supported by W. Edwards Deming's insight that cooperation between components is crucial to achieving the organization's aim.

## Practical Approaches to Identifying Bottlenecks
- The Theory of Constraints thinking can be applied practically by identifying the bottleneck in the system, which is often not individual developer speed, but rather slow code reviews, unclear requirements, or poor cross-team communication, and focusing improvement efforts there.
- The use of AI coding assistants can be valuable for repetitive tasks and exploration, but it is dangerous when used uncritically on complex work, and it is crucial to invest in practices that AI will amplify, such as fixing feedback loops, reducing cognitive load, and protecting the flow state, before introducing AI into a system.

## Reframing the Question: Conditions Over Metrics
- When an organization asks about measuring developer productivity, it is worth examining the motivation behind the question, as it can lead to different approaches, including systems thinking, honest conversations about individual performance or organizational dysfunction, and outcome measurement that connects engineering work to business results.
- The real question that organizations should be asking is not "How productive are our developers?" but rather "How do we create the conditions where developers can do their best work?", which requires leadership to engage with the complexity and creativity of software development and cannot be answered by a single number or dashboard.
- This approach is supported by examples such as Bill Atkinson's experience in 1982, where he made software six times faster by writing 2,000 fewer lines of code, demonstrating that productivity is not just about metrics, but about achieving meaningful outcomes, a concept that is particularly important in the age of AI, where the temptation to optimize bad metrics at machine speed is greater than ever.

## Introduction to Non-Violent Communication
- The late psychologist [[marshall-rosenberg|Marshall Rosenberg]] developed a practical strategy for peaceful conflict resolution called [[non-violent-communication-the-best-way-to-connect|non-violent communication]], which focuses on language and process to shift the tone of communication and spur collaboration, and this method is now used by companies, conflict negotiators, and personal therapists.
- Rosenberg's non-violent communication method is rooted in the belief that all humans share the same universal needs, including the sense that they are being heard, understood, valued, and respected, and conflicts arise when words are perceived as threats, which devolve into power struggles.
- Dian Killian, a certified trainer in Rosenberg's method, breaks down the four steps to meaningful conversations, which include observing and recapping, describing emotions, identifying needs, and connecting about everyone's needs, with the goal of connecting about everyone's needs, not to "win" the conversation.

## The Four Steps of Meaningful Conversations
- The first step, observe and recap, involves neutral observation and recapping what someone has said without emotional input, using comments that begin in the first person, such as "I hear you say…", to slow the pace of conversation and force both sides to reflect and clarify.
- The second step, describe emotions, not positions, involves talking feelings, not issues, and clearly describing one's own emotions, rather than policy positions, using phrases such as "Are you feeling frightened and disrespected?" to express emotional turmoil without translating emotions into blame.
- The third step, identify needs, involves pairing expressed feelings with unmet needs, which are requirements for contentment, and according to Rosenberg, human needs universally fall into categories such as connection, honesty, peace, play, physical well-being, a sense of meaning, and autonomy.

## Outcomes and Benefits of the Method
- By following these steps, individuals can shift the tone of their communication and spur collaboration, even in contentious conversations, and develop a "muscle" for meaningful conversation that can be used in a variety of contexts, including personal and professional relationships.

## Breakthrough Conversations Across Differences
- The process of having a breakthrough conversation across party lines involves several key steps, including identifying needs, making a connection request, and making a concrete request, as outlined by experts such as Killian and Rosenberg, who emphasize the importance of understanding and empathy in these conversations.
- Returning to identifying needs can help remove roadblocks in heated conversations, and typically, around 80 percent of the time when both people are triggered, it is necessary to focus on a connection request before moving on, which can be achieved by asking questions like "I'm wondering if I understand correctly how you're feeling concerned" or "I'm wondering what you've heard me say".
- Making a request is a crucial step in the conversation, where one party asks for concrete actions that would help satisfy a need, and this request should arise organically when both sides are openly connecting, such as asking someone to read an article about immigrants and the economy, and it is essential to make this request in a moment of understanding between the parties.

## Managing Inner Conflict and Empathy
- The four steps outlined in the process can also be used to manage one's own needs and inner conflict, by noticing what is triggering you, naming your judgment and feelings about that trigger, recognizing the need connected to that judgment, and finding a strategy to help you cope with that need, as explained by Killian, who advises patience and practice to become "fluent" in this practice, which is similar to learning a second language.
- According to Killian, if you are telling yourself that someone is "ignorant", you are probably looking for awareness and shared reality, and if you believe someone is "difficult", you are actually wanting a sense of connection, and Rosenberg notes that one should not expect someone who has been injured to hear their side until they feel that they have been fully understood, highlighting the importance of empathy and understanding in these conversations.

## Practical Application and Challenges
- The practice of having breakthrough conversations requires patience, self-awareness, and a willingness to understand the other person's perspective, and beginners may want to start by limiting their practice to the first step, which is to recap and identify their intention in conversations, and as Killian warns, when someone's needs are not being met, they may become more violent in language or action, emphasizing the need for empathy and understanding in these conversations.

## The Problem with Output Thinking
- The concept of "ship faster" can be misleading, as teams may be shipping more than ever, but everything is getting slower, and this slowdown can be difficult to detect and misdiagnose, especially as systems grow faster and decisions become more compressed.
- As teams produce more visible work, the temptation to equate motion with meaning grows stronger, and output measures activity, whereas outcome measures change, leading to a situation where teams become very good at looking productive, even as real progress slows.
- A team can ship multiple features without improving the user experience, close hundreds of tickets without reducing support volume, and move fast without moving the business forward, illustrating the difference between output and outcome, where "output accumulates, but outcomes compound".
- The problem with output thinking is that it is like a payday loan, feeling amazing at first, but eventually, the bill shows up with interest, as complexity has calcified, engineers who understand the core system have left or burned out, and new hires take a long time to merge their first real pull request.
- Leaders often misinterpret this stage, calling it scale or maturity, but it is actually the compounded cost of trading judgment for output, where people respond to what the system rewards, and if engineers are praised for shipping quickly, they will ship quickly, even if it means sacrificing meaningful work.
- Over time, teams learn the wrong lessons, such as making work look hard, not valuable, picking battles that can be won fast, skipping difficult architectural problems, and not asking questions that might slow them down, leading to a situation where everyone stays busy, but the system quietly rots.

- The example of an engineer named Viraj, who was shipping more code than the rest of the team combined, illustrates the problem, as his commit graph looked impressive, but it did not necessarily translate to meaningful work or real progress.
- The section from the document 'The Three-Year Bill: Why Fast Teams Slow Down' discusses how teams that prioritize speed over understanding can eventually slow down due to the accumulation of context debt, which is the gap in shared mental models that reveals itself when changes are needed.

Vengono presentati anche strumenti e tecnologie di controllo della versione (VCS) che facilitano TBD.

**Temi Principali e Idee Chiave:**

1. **Definizione e vantaggi del Trunk-Based Development:**
- TBD è una pratica di sviluppo software in cui gli sviluppatori integrano regolarmente il proprio codice in un ramo principale condiviso (il "trunk" o "mainline").
- L'obiettivo principale è ridurre la "distanza" tra gli sviluppatori, promuovendo una collaborazione più frequente e riducendo i conflitti di merge.
- I vantaggi includono:
- Release più frequenti e più piccole.
- Feedback più rapido e miglioramento continuo.
- Riduzione dei rischi associati a branch di lunga durata.
- Maggiore visibilità e trasparenza.
1. **Branch by Abstraction (BBA):**
- BBA è una tecnica per introdurre grandi cambiamenti o nuove funzionalità in un sistema esistente senza creare branch di lunga durata.
- L'approccio consiste nell'introduzione di un'astrazione (un'interfaccia o un'API) che consente sia il vecchio che il nuovo comportamento di coesistere.
- Una volta che la nuova implementazione è completa e testata, si può passare completamente alla nuova versione e rimuovere la vecchia.
- "Yes, by abstraction instead of by branching in source control. And no, that doesn’t mean sprinkle conditionals into your source code, it means to use an abstraction concept that’s idiomatic for the programming language you are using."
1. **Stili di Trunk-Based Development:**
- Il documento identifica tre stili principali:
- **Committing Straight to the Trunk:** Gli sviluppatori committano direttamente nel trunk dopo un build locale e test di successo.
- **Short-Lived Feature Branches:** Gli sviluppatori lavorano su branch di feature di breve durata (in genere della durata di uno o due giorni) che vengono poi mergeate nel trunk.
- **Coupled "Patch Review" System:** Un sistema di code review pre-commit come Mondrian (usato da Google) che permette la validazione del codice *prima* che venga committato sul trunk.
1. **Feature Flags (o Feature Toggles):**
- I Feature Flags sono meccanismi per attivare o disattivare funzionalità a runtime senza richiedere un redeploy.
- Permettono di:
- Rilasciare funzionalità in modo incrementale.
- Eseguire A/B testing.
- Disattivare funzionalità problematiche in produzione.
- Personalizzare l'esperienza utente.
- È importante gestire attentamente i Feature Flags per evitare "tech debt" (debito tecnico).
- "Flags, which get incorporated into codebases over time, often get forgotten as devel-opment teams pivot towards new business deliverables... The remediation of flags (and the code to which they apply) a month after the release is good business practice and should be recommended."
1. **Continuous Integration (CI) e Continuous Delivery (CD):**
- CI è il processo di integrare frequentemente il codice nel trunk e di eseguire build e test automatizzati per verificare la correttezza del codice.
- CD è l'estensione di CI che automatizza il rilascio del software in ambienti di test o di produzione.
- "Individuals practice Trunk-Based Development, and teams practice CI".
- Le pipeline CI devono gestire le permutazioni di feature flag. "It is necessary for CI to guard your anticipated permutations of flags."
1. **Scelta del Sistema di Controllo della Versione (VCS):**
- La scelta del VCS è importante per supportare efficacemente TBD.
- Git e Mercurial sono popolari, ma anche Perforce, Subversion, Team Foundation Server (TFS) e PlasticSCM possono essere usati con successo.
- Caratteristiche importanti da considerare includono:
- Velocità di update/pull/sync. "The time taken to effect the update/pull/sync should be less than three seconds".
- Supporto per branching e merging lightweight.
- Permessi granulari (importante per le grandi aziende).
1. **Monorepos:**
- Un monorepo è un singolo repository che contiene il codice per più progetti o componenti.
- I monorepos possono semplificare la condivisione del codice, la gestione delle dipendenze e la refactoring.
- Google è un famoso esempio di azienda che utilizza un monorepo con TBD.
- Tecnologie come [gcheckout.sh](http://gcheckout.sh) (Google), e funzionalità come "sparse checkout" in Git, Perforce client-specs, e PlasticSCM's cloaked.conf permettono agli sviluppatori di lavorare su un sottoinsieme del monorepo, riducendo il tempo di checkout e il consumo di risorse.
1. **Bottlenecks Comuni e Soluzioni:**
- Il documento identifica diversi bottlenecks comuni che possono ostacolare l'adozione di TBD, tra cui:
- Build lenti.
- Mancanza di test automatizzati.
- Cultura di sviluppo che resiste al cambiamento.
- Soluzioni proposte includono:
- Ottimizzare i tempi di build.
- Implementare un'adeguata strategia di test (unit test, integration test).
- Promuovere una cultura di collaborazione e di ownership condivisa del codice.
1. **"You're Doing It Wrong":**
- Il documento evidenzia errori comuni che le aziende commettono quando tentano di implementare TBD, come ad esempio:
- Semplicemente chiamare un branch "trunk" senza adottare le pratiche TBD.
- Usare branch di feature di lunga durata.
- Non cancellare i branch dopo il merge.
- Cherry-picking di bug fix dai branch di release al trunk (invece di fixare il trunk direttamente).
1. **Cultura e Fattori Umani:**
- L'adozione di TBD richiede un cambiamento culturale all'interno del team di sviluppo.
- È importante promuovere la psychological safety, la collaborazione e la comunicazione.
- "Culture eats strategy for breakfast." Un buon piano strategico può fallire se la cultura aziendale non lo supporta.
**Citazioni Significative:**

- "If it is not done in source-control it is not worth doing". (Sull'importanza del controllo della versione)
- "Individuals practice Trunk-Based Development, and teams practice CI". (Sul rapporto tra TBD e CI)
- "Culture eats strategy for breakfast". (Sull'importanza della cultura aziendale)
**Conclusioni:**

Trunk-Based Development è una pratica potente che può aiutare le aziende a migliorare la velocità, la qualità e l'affidabilità del rilascio del software. Tuttavia, l'implementazione di TBD richiede un'attenta pianificazione, una solida infrastruttura e un cambiamento culturale all'interno del team di sviluppo. Il "Branch by Abstraction" è una tecnica utile per facilitare cambiamenti importanti senza incorrere nelle problematiche dei branch di lunga durata.

## Struttura e Composizione dei Team
- Le moderne organizzazioni IT devono bilanciare stabilità e velocità, e il lavoro in team è fondamentale per raggiungere questo obiettivo.
- I team non devono essere considerati come semplici collezioni di persone, ma come sistemi "socio-tecnici" che richiedono autonomia e una chiara visione.
- La legge di [[melvin-conway|Conway]] afferma che le organizzazioni che disegnano sistemi sono vincolate a produrre design che sono copie della loro struttura comunicativa, e quindi è importante definire strutture di team che siano precursori dell'architettura desiderata.
- Il carico cognitivo è un concetto importante da considerare, poiché esiste un limite alla quantità di informazioni che possono essere contenute in un cervello umano o in un team, e quindi è necessario contenere il carico cognitivo per evitare colli di bottiglia.

## Gestione del Carico Cognitivo
- Le organizzazioni dovrebbero minimizzare il carico cognitivo intrinseco e eliminare il carico cognitivo esterno per lasciare spazio per il carico cognitivo attinente, dove si genera creatività e valore.
- È importante valutare il carico cognitivo di un team chiedendo se si sentono efficaci e in grado di eseguire il lavoro richiesto, e non utilizzare misure semplici come le linee di codice o il numero di classi.
- La comunicazione non deve essere eccessiva, secondo la legge di [[melvin-conway|Conway]], e dovrebbe essere focalizzata e consapevolmente definita, come suggerito da Henrik Kniberg, che propone di limitare la larghezza di banda comunicativa tra team non accoppiati.

## Dimensionamento e Autonomia dei Team
- La scelta degli strumenti di lavoro e degli ambienti di lavoro può influenzare la comunicazione e dovrebbe essere fatta in modo da agevolare la collaborazione e la produttività, come suggerito da [[fred-brooks|Fred Brooks]] con la sua legge sul costo della comunicazione.
- Le organizzazioni devono investire nel miglioramento delle pratiche dei team di tecnologia, come la Continuous Delivery, Test-first Development, operability, affidabilità e security, per raggiungere l'eccellenza tecnica e il buon design.
- I team devono essere composti da un minimo di 5 a un massimo di 9 persone, come teorizzato dal Numero di Dunbar di [[robin-dunbar|Robin Dunbar]], per massimizzare la fiducia e la collaborazione.

## Principi Agili e Organizzativi
- I team devono avere ownership del prodotto o servizio a cui lavorano e non dovrebbero esserci componenti con ownership condivisa, come sottolineato da Allan Kelly, per evitare problemi di responsabilità e conflitti.
- Le organizzazioni tradizionali che adottano Metodologie Agili devono evitare di creare team volatili e silos funzionali, ma invece enfatizzare un rapido flusso di cambiamenti e produzione di valore.
- È importante mantenere i team il più autonomi possibile, riducendo le dipendenze esterne e garantendo che le eventuali dipendenze non siano bloccanti.

## Team Topologies e Tipologie di Team
- Team Topologies propone 4 tipologie di team: [[stream-aligned-team|Stream-Aligned Team]], Platform Team, Enabling Team e Complicated-subsystem Team, che dovrebbero agire come calamite per tutti i team dell'organizzazione.
- I team Stream-Aligned sono il principale tipo di team e sono allineati a un singolo flusso di lavoro che produce valore per l'utente finale, con lo scopo di ridurre il carico cognitivo e di incorporare rapidamente il feedback del cliente.
- I team "Stream-Aligned" sono caratterizzati da una produzione costante di funzionalità in produzione, rapida correzione di rotta e uso di un approccio sperimentale.
- Gli "Enabling Team" aiutano i team "Stream-Aligned" a colmare le lacune di conoscenza, fornendo assistenza e orientamento senza eseguire lavoro, e applicano la Servant Leadership.
- I "Complicated-Subsystem Team" si occupano di realizzare e mantenere parti del sistema che richiedono conoscenze specialistiche, riducendo il carico cognitivo dei team "Stream-Aligned".
- I "Platform Team" forniscono servizi interni per ridurre il carico cognitivo dei team "Stream-Aligned", abilitandoli a generare valore con sostanziale autonomia.

## Conversione e Adattamento dei Team
- Le piattaforme possono essere composte da gruppi di team di diverse tipologie e richiedono capacità di product management per evitare di diventare troppo complesse.
- È importante convertire team esistenti in una delle 4 topologie di Team Topologies (Stream-Aligned, Platform, Enabling, Complicated-Subsystem) per migliorare la flessibilità e l'efficienza.

## Fratturazione del Monolite
- I team di supporto dovrebbero essere accoppiati ai team Stream-Aligned e utilizzare il modello di swarming per la risoluzione degli incidenti.
- Il monolite può essere affettato utilizzando piani di frattura come i bounded context del dominio di business, la compliance regolatoria e la frequenza di cambiamento per creare sistemi più autonomi e indipendenti.
- La scelta del piano di frattura per spezzare il monolite in sotto-sistemi può essere basata su diversi criteri, come l'ubicazione dei team, il rischio, le performance, le user personas e la tecnologia.
- È importante evitare di separare il lavoro di manutenzione del vecchio sistema da quello di costruzione del nuovo, poiché ciò può portare alla perdita di feedback e alla riduzione della possibilità di costruire un prodotto migliore.

## Modalità di Interazione tra Team
- Le modalità di interazione tra team definite da Team Topologies sono la Collaborazione, X-as-a-service e la Facilitazione, e possono aiutare a migliorare l'efficacia del lavoro dei team e a ridurre la frustrazione.
- La Collaborazione è adatta a situazioni complesse e incerte, mentre X-as-a-service è più adatta a situazioni in cui è necessaria una delivery predicibile.
- La scelta della modalità di interazione giusta può aiutare a migliorare la chiarezza degli scopi, l'engagement e a ridurre la frustrazione nei rapporti tra i team.
- La modalità X-as-a-service è adatta quando il servizio fornito richiede poca interazione tra il team fornitore e il team consumatore, e il team fornitore deve avere una forte responsabilità verso i suoi consumatori.
- La modalità Facilitazione è la modalità principale di lavoro per gli Enabling Team, che aiutano altri team attraverso coaching, mentoring e facilitazione, senza prendere parte alla realizzazione del sistema.
- Le 4 topologie di team (Stream-Aligned, Platform, Complicated-Subsystem e Enabling) hanno comportamenti caratteristici e possono adottare diverse modalità di interazione (X-as-a-service, Collaborazione e Facilitazione) per migliorare l'outcome dell'organizzazione, come suggerito da Matthew Skelton e Manuel Pais.

- Fornire una visione di business chiara e una direzione per tutta l'organizzazione con obiettivi chiari e realistici
